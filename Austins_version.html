1. Data Description

After the code block(s) that read in and clean the data, add some notes covering:

What does each row represent? What is the unit of observation?

  • Each row represents one Indonesian district in one year in . The unit of observation is a district-year. The dataset covers 201 districts observed every year from 1998 to 2014, which is 17 years. So in total there are 201 × 17 = 3,417 rows.

What are the key outcome, treatment, and control variables?

  • Outcome Variables - The study measures conflict using the total number of conflict incidents per district per year. These are the primary outcomes used in Tables 3 and 8. This comes from the National Violence Monitoring System, which records conflict events reported in Indonesian provincial newspapers. The authors also examine six conflict subtypes separately in Table 4 resource disputes (land and water conflicts), popular justice, law enforcement incidents, government program conflicts, separatist movements, and identity or ethnic conflicts. The split between economic conflict types and political/identity types is central to the paper’s argument.

  • Treatment - The main variable is growing season rainfall, measured as a within district z-score. This means it captures how much rainy or drier a given year was compared to that district’s own historical average, not whether one district is wetter than another. The key variable of interest is the interaction between this rainfall shock and baseline irrigation capacity, which is fixed at 1997 levels. A positive coefficient on this interaction means that districts with more irrigation experience a smaller increase in conflict during drought years, which is the paper’s central finding.

  • Control Variable - The models include growing season temperature and its interaction with irrigation capacity as direct controls, since temperature also affects crop yields and may be correlated with rainfall. The authors also include district fixed effects to absorb all time-invariant differences across districts, year fixed effects to control for nationwide shocks in any given year, and province-specific linear time trends to account for the fact that some provinces were already trending toward more or less conflict over the sample period for reasons unrelated to weather. These controls together ensure the rainfall coefficient is identified from year to year shocks within districts, not from cross sectional differences between them.

Any notable data cleaning decisions (dropped observations, constructed variables, etc.)

  • Observations from Jakarta and Yogyakarta were dropped since agriculture is not relevant in these places.

  • The median agric_per_capita is used to split the data into 2 sub-samples agric_data (Agricultural) and non_agric_data (Non-Agricultural). Samples below the median are non_agric_data (Non-Agricultural) and samples above or equal to the median are agric_data (Agricultural).

Sample size, time period, and geographic scope

  • This study has 3,417 district-year observations across 201 districts. The time period is from 1998-2014 (17 years). The geographic scope of this study is Indonesia (excluding Jakarta and Yogyakarta).

2. Annotated Replication

What does the code accomplish? (summary of the procedure or model)

This code runs our primary fixed effects OLS model that estimates how rainfall and irrigation infrastructure affect conflict in our 201 districts. There are 4 different models; a baseline, and 3 modifications that bring detail to the relationship and provide a pseudo robustness check. The main model m3_c1 is our baseline relationship model. This code estimates whether growing-season rainfall affects conflict incidents, and whether irrigation infrastructure buffers that effect, while controlling for temperature, district fixed effects, year fixed effects, and province-specific time trends. The | district_code accounts for fixed differences across districts, factor(year) controls for nationwide shocks in each year, and year:factor(prov) allows each province to follow individual time trends — this is to ensure the rainfall-conflict relationship isn’t being driven by other variables. The second model m3_c2 provides stricter controls, looking at the same relationship but each district has it’s own time trend, rather than a single time trend across all districts like in the baseline model. This allows the model to account for district level trends in conflict that may not be due to rainfall. The third model m3_c3 replaces the 1997 baseline irrigation with current year irrigation z_rain_irrigation_cont as a robustness check. If the pattern holds with both sets of irrigation data, we can have more confidence that irrigation is influencing conflict. The fourth model, m3_c4 replaces rainfall with rice production ihs_total_prod as the mechanism driving conflict. If irrigation is mitigating conflict by stabilizing agricultural income, then lower rice production should predict more conflict - so this last model asks whether the rainfall –> conflict pathway passes through agriculture. The subsequent pieces of code produce the results table. The last part of the code counts the number of districts in irrigation and makes a data frame that assigns the number of districts to each OLS, to add the number of districts into the summary table.

What is the causal identification approach? (e.g., DiD, RDD, IV, matching)

  • The causal identification approach is Fixed Effects OLS. Simplified, the belief is that if you control for district fixed effects, year fixed effects, and province-specific time trends, the relationship between rainfall and conflict can be interpreted as causal.

What assumptions must hold for the estimates to be causally valid?

  • Rainfall must be totally exogenous - meaning that districts must not be able to manipulate or anticipate rainfall shocks.

  • Irrigation infrastructure must be unrelated to other factors driving conflict - if hisitorically existing irrigation infrastructure influenced conflict trends in districts over time, the effect of irrigation could be reduced/confounded in the current time period by pre-existing differences.

  • Parallel trends: districts would have followed similar conflict trajectories as eachother in the absence of rainfall variation.

  • OVB: There cannot be omitted time-varying variables in districts that are correlated with both rainfall shock and conflict (other than those accounted for with FE)

Are there any signs in the output that those assumptions are or are not satisfied?

Nothing in the output stands out as a specific violation of any of our assumptions. Our results show that there is an insignificant effect of rainfall on conflict. With Rainfall X Irrigation interaction we see a significant effect, but the fact that rainfall alone doesn’t have a significant effect indicates that the Rainfall x Conflict relationship is weaker than expected, and there may be another causation path into agriculture x conflict relationship. Also, a positive coefficient on the Rainfall x Irrigation interaction indicates that higher rainfall actually reduces the conflict-mitigating effect of irrigation - so basically irrigation matters most when rainfall is low.  These results are somewhat unexpected - but they don’t show any obvious violations of our assumptions.

3. Critical Evaluation Somewhere in the write-up (or as a closing section), address each of the following:

Causal identification: Is the identification strategy credible? What would have to be true for the estimates to be causal?

The identification strategy is credible. Rainfall goes up and down from year to year because of El Nino, a global weather pattern that has nothing to do with what happens in any Indonesian district. This is what makes it a good natural experiment, nobody controls the rain.

For the results to show a true cause and effect relationship, four conditions must be met. First, rainfall shocks must be random with respect to local conditions, which is reasonable since El Nino operates at a global scale. Second, districts that had more irrigation in 1997 must not be different from less irrigated districts in other ways that also affect how they respond to drought. Third, all districts must have been on similar conflict trends before the study period. Fourth, conflict in one district must not simply move to a neighboring district when drought hits, which would make the effect look smaller than it really is.

Statistical assumptions: Are key assumptions (parallel trends, exclusion restriction, etc.) tested or defended? Do you find the evidence convincing?

The authors cannot directly test parallel trends because the conflict data only starts in 1998, the same year the study begins. So there is no earlier period to check whether irrigated and non-irrigated districts were already moving in different directions before the sample. This is a genuine gap. They show that the results barely change when district-specific time trends are added (Table 3, Column 2), which suggests the findings are not driven by some districts already trending toward less conflict.

For the exclusion restriction, the assumption that irrigation only affects conflict through the agricultural income channel and not through some other path, the authors provide two pieces of supporting evidence. They also run the same regression separately for each conflict type (Table 4) and show that the irrigation effect only shows up for economically motivated conflicts, not for ethnic or separatist ones. If irrigation were simply picking up the effect of being a wealthier or better governed district, we would expect it to reduce all types of conflict, not just the economic ones. Second, the hydropower placebo test (not replicated here) shows that dam capacity used for electricity generation, which has similar placement patterns as irrigation but no farming function, does not reduce conflict. This strongly suggests the result is specifically about agriculture, not about dams or development in general.

Overall the evidence is fairly convincing, but not perfect. The missing pre-trend test and the possibility that irrigated districts differ from non-irrigated ones in ways we cannot observe are the two main remaining concerns.

Sampling & external validity: Who is in the sample? To whom can results be generalized?

  • The sample in this study are 201 Indonesian districts observed from 1998-2014. Jakarta and Yogyakarta are excluded due to agriculture not being relevant in these areas. Even though this study has a low generalizability, these results can be generalized to other rural agricultural districts in developing countries with government-managed irrigation.

Measurement: Are the outcome and treatment variables measured accurately and without systematic bias?

  • The treatment variables (Rainfall and irrigation) are measured correctly. The outcome variable (conflict) has sample limitations. The NVMS data (conflict data) comes from newspaper reports, since agricultural districts are more rural they have less media coverage. This causes agricultural districts’ conflicts to be under counted where irrigation effect should be strongest.

Other limitations: Anything else that affects how much weight you would put on the study’s conclusions?

  • Indonesia has a very unique political history, crop structure, and government-owned irrigation system that may not apply to other developing countries. This makes the study have a low generalizability.

  • A limitation is that they can only estimate the coefficient associated with the interaction between irrigation capacity and rainfall but not the direct effect of an increase in irrigation capacity on agricultural production in years with zero rainfall. So, They can not rule out that the lower slope of the rainfall–production relationship in districts with high irrigation capacity is because irrigation leads to lower agricultural productivity at high rainfall levels, though this appears unlikely in practice.
